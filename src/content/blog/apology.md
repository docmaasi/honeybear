Most apologies fail. Not because the person was insincere, but because they apologised for the wrong thing, defended themselves halfway through, or wanted the matter closed before the other person had finished being hurt.

There is a structure that works. It is not complicated, and it is uncomfortable, which is why so few people use it.

## Why apologies fail

**The apology is for the reaction, not the act.** *I'm sorry you were upset* is not an apology. It relocates the problem into the other person.

**A defence gets attached.** Everything before the word *but* is deleted by everything after it.

**It arrives with a deadline.** An apology delivered in order to end a conversation is a transaction, and people can feel the difference instantly.

**It describes the wrong injury.** Apologising for being late when the hurt was about being deprioritised means the actual wound goes unacknowledged.

## What works

**1. Name what you did, specifically.** Not *I messed up*. The actual thing. *I said I would call and I didn't, and then I made it sound like you were being demanding for minding.*

**2. Name the impact, in their words not yours.** *You spent the evening feeling like an inconvenience.* Getting this right is most of the work, and it means you have to have listened rather than rehearsed.

**3. No explanation unless invited.** This is the hard one. Context feels essential to you and reads as a defence to them. If they want it, they will ask. They usually do, later, once they believe you understand.

**4. Say what changes.** Not *I'll do better*. Something checkable. *I'm putting it in my calendar so it doesn't depend on me remembering.*

**5. Give up control of the timing.** You do not get to decide when it is resolved. Wanting that is understandable; requiring it undoes the apology.

## What not to say

- *I said I was sorry.* — Repetition is not repair.
- *You do it too.* — Possibly true, entirely separate conversation.
- *I can't do anything right.* — Now they are comforting you about your apology.
- *Can we move on?* — Not your call.

## Receiving one

If you are on the other end, two things help.

**Say what you actually need.** Most people do not know, and the other person cannot guess. *I need you to check in before you agree to things that affect both of us* is usable. *I need you to care more* is not.

**If you accept it, actually accept it.** Keeping it in reserve to deploy in three weeks means the repair never completes, and the relationship slowly fills with unfinished business.

## Repair as a skill

Every long relationship is a very long chain of small ruptures. What distinguishes the ones that last is not fewer ruptures — it is a shorter distance between rupture and repair.

That is learnable. Many people never learned it because nobody in their childhood modelled it; if the adults around you either exploded or went silent for three days, you had no template. Katherine writes about that inheritance in **Weight of Dysfunction**, [available on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Weight-Dysfunction-Katherine-L-Carter/dp/1667889222).

You can learn it now. It is mostly practice, and it works even when only one of you starts.
