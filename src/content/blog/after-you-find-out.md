The first week is not for decisions. It is for staying upright.

People make enormous, permanent choices in the first seventy-two hours — leaving, forgiving, telling everyone, telling no one — while running on no sleep and a nervous system that has been kicked over. Almost all of them wish they had waited.

Here is what the first week is actually for.

## What is happening to you

Betrayal produces something close to a trauma response, and it is useful to know that so you stop grading yourself on it.

Sleep breaks. Appetite goes. Concentration disappears. You may swing from icy calm to fury within a minute. You will replay months of memory looking for what you missed. You may want them badly and want them gone in the same hour.

None of that means you are unstable. It means something structural was removed and your mind is trying to rebuild the floor.

## The five things worth doing

**1. Tell one person.** Exactly one, chosen for discretion rather than loyalty. Isolation makes this materially worse. Telling twelve people creates a chorus you will later have to manage.

**2. Eat and sleep, badly if necessary.** This is not trivial advice. Every decision you make while unslept will be worse than the one you would make rested.

**3. Write down what you already know.** Not to build a case. Because memory reorganises itself under stress, and you will want an accurate record later.

**4. Postpone the permanent.** No moving out, no telling the children, no messaging the other person, no announcements. Say it out loud: *I am not deciding anything this week.*

**5. Ask for one thing only: honesty from here.** Not the full account today. A commitment that there will be no more discoveries. Repair is impossible on a drip feed, and staggered revelations do more long-term damage than the original event.

## What not to do

**Do not interrogate at three in the morning.** You will get answers shaped to end the conversation.

**Do not demand every detail immediately.** Some detail is necessary. Complete forensic reconstruction is usually self-harm, and it cannot be unlearned.

**Do not contact the other person.** It has never once helped.

**Do not decide who you are now.** Not a fool, not a failure. You trusted someone, which is what people who are capable of love do.

## What you are allowed

You are allowed to still love them. You are allowed to want them and be disgusted at the same time. You are allowed to not know. None of that is weakness or hypocrisy — it is what the middle of this looks like from the inside.

## Then what

After the first week, the question is not *do I forgive this?* It is narrower and more answerable: **can this person be honest with me now, and do they understand what they actually did?**

If they can and they do, repair is possible. Slower than either of you wants, but real. [How to apologise so it lands](/blog/apology) is where that starts.

If the response is minimising, defending, or blaming you for the state of the relationship, you have important information — and you did not have to go looking for it.

If any part of the situation involves fear rather than grief, read [early signs of a controlling relationship](/blog/control-signs), because that is a different problem with a different answer.
