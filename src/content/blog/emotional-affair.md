Almost nobody decides to have an emotional affair. That is precisely what makes them common. They begin as a friendship that happens to be unusually easy, with someone who happens to find you interesting at a time when nobody else seems to.

There is a path, and it is remarkably consistent.

## The stages

**It is easy.** Conversation flows. You are funny with this person. You notice you look forward to it, and you think nothing of it, because so far there is nothing to think.

**It becomes the first place things go.** Good news, bad news, the odd thought at lunchtime. This is the real turning point, and it passes unnoticed almost every time. Being told first is what intimacy is made of, and it has quietly changed address.

**You begin editing.** Not lying. Just not mentioning. You leave the conversation out of the account of your day, and you would struggle to explain exactly why.

**The comparison starts.** They listen properly. They are not tired or irritable. You start noticing what is missing at home — and you are comparing someone's best three hours a week against someone's entire life, which is not a fair fight and never was.

**The friendship gets defended.** If your partner raises it, you feel indignant. That indignation is worth examining rather than trusting. Nobody defends a genuinely ordinary friendship that hard.

## The two questions

**Does your partner know how much of you this person gets?** Not that they exist. How much they get.

**What are you no longer bringing home?** Emotional affairs are not additions. They are transfers.

## Why they hurt so much

Because they cannot be dismissed as a mistake. A drunken lapse is a bad decision. Eight months of choosing someone every day is a **preference**, expressed repeatedly, in private.

And there is a second injury underneath: the discovery that your partner had a whole interior life you were not in. Many people find that harder to recover from than the disloyalty itself.

## If you are in one

You do not need to have crossed a line for this to be worth stopping. Ask what need it is meeting — being found interesting, being wanted, being new to someone — and then ask whether you have ever asked for that at home, in plain words, recently.

Most people have not. They have hinted, or they asked years ago and gave up. That is worth returning to before the alternative decides things for you.

If the friendship cannot survive your partner knowing the full shape of it, you already know what it is.

## If your partner is in one

Lead with the loss, not the evidence. *"I have felt like I'm getting what's left over"* starts a conversation. A list of timestamps starts a trial, and trials end with a verdict rather than a marriage.

Expect defensiveness first. It usually is not proof of guilt; it is the sound of someone realising something they had avoided naming.

## Where the pattern often comes from

People who learned early that closeness was conditional, or that peace was kept by never saying the true thing, are unusually prone to this.
